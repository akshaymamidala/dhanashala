Why this exists

Most students don't choose a career. They drift into one.

During my B.Tech I explored digital marketing, finance, stock markets, software development, HR and several other fields. I spent a lot of time trying directions without ever reaching a clear milestone.

I regret not having proper career direction earlier. I don't want other students to go through the same experience — so this is being built to give them what I did not have.

You should not have to spend three years finding out what you are good at.

  • Nobody tells you what is hiring

    Which domains are growing, which are saturated, which skills recur across every listing — students pick a specialisation on the basis of a friend's advice or a brochure.

  • Certificates are not proof

    A course provider sells a certificate. An employer wants to see that you can actually do the work: the project, the write-up, the thing you can explain in an interview.

  • The advice arrives far too late

    Career guidance usually lands in final year, from people who have not hired anyone this cycle. By then it is a scramble, not a direction.

The ecosystem

Five stages, one path

Each stage is a step. Start at the first and keep going — or join wherever you already are. Stages three to five are being built, not sold.

What this stage is

Long-form subject lessons built for students who want marks and understanding, not just a passing grade. Free and public, so an audience and a community form before any product does.

What you walk away with

Notes you can revise from, a stronger grip on the subject, and a channel you can put in front of an employer as evidence you teach yourself in public.

What this stage is

Playlists and learning paths, each one mapped to a specific role rather than a broad “full-stack” umbrella. The point is to show students what a job description actually asks for, and where their gap is.

What you walk away with

A shortlist of domains you can defend with reasons, and a named skills gap for each one — instead of a vague feeling about which direction “feels right”.

What this stage is

The point where a student stops being a viewer and starts producing. Shipped projects with real review, a resume that survives a six-second scan, LinkedIn and professional profile work, and interview practice with feedback.

What you walk away with

A portfolio that starts conversations and a career narrative you can say out loud without rehearsing it. The goal is not to finish a course — it is to be job-ready.

What this stage is

A job portal built for students, fed by the same pipeline that runs the programmes. Trained students work as interns researching the Indian job market and maintaining the platform itself — they are the users building it.

What you walk away with

Applications that were still open when you sent them. Listings carry an expiry and remove themselves at the closing date, so nobody wastes a week on a role that has already gone.

What this stage is

Domain discovery, skill development and real projects started in year one — three or four years before the first placement season, instead of final-year panic.

What you walk away with

A direction you chose, with a body of evidence behind it. By the time recruiters look at you, the choice has already been tested against real work.

The output

What a student walks away with

Not a certificate. Six things that are still true a year later, whatever route you came in by.

Academic foundation

  • Better marks

    Teaching aimed at the exam and at the understanding underneath it.

  • Notes that survive

    Revisable material instead of a playlist you never finish.

  • Doubt support

    Questions answered before they turn into a lost week.

Job-relevant skills

  • Role-mapped tracks

    Each path written against real job descriptions, not a course catalogue.

  • Tool-level fluency

    Enough depth to be useful on a team, not enough to be a certificate.

  • A defensible shortlist

    Two or three domains you can justify, with the gap named for each.

Practical proof

  • Real projects

    Work with a brief, a deadline and something at the end of it.

  • Reviewed, not graded

    Someone reads it and tells you what a hiring manager would say.

  • 1:1 project guidance

    For the part where you are stuck and the tutorial ran out.

Career readiness

  • Mock interviews

    Rehearsed with feedback, including the questions you cannot prepare for.

  • Resume building

    Built to be read in six seconds by someone who has read a thousand.

  • LinkedIn & profile

    A professional presence that matches the work, not just the degree.

Digital portfolio

  • A portfolio site

    One link that shows the work instead of describing it.

  • Written case studies

    The thinking behind the project, which is what gets asked about.

  • Proof, not certificates

    Things a recruiter can open and judge for themselves.

Placement support

  • Internship pipeline

    Entry points that do not ask for three years of experience.

  • Referrals that mean something

    Introductions through teams that have hired from the programme.

  • Mentorship & guidance

    Someone who has done the job and has time to answer.

The problem we are actually fixing

Listings that close themselves.

Applying to a job that closed three weeks ago is the most common way students lose a week. On Dhanashala, a listing carries its closing date and is removed the moment it passes — nobody has to remember to tidy up.

  • Expiry set at publish

    The closing date is part of the listing, not an afterthought added by a recruiter.

  • Removed automatically

    When the clock runs out the listing leaves search, and the application button goes with it.

  • No dead listings to click

    Students only ever see roles a recruiter still intends to fill.
